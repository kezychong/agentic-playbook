# The creative brief

This file is for the agent. It gives you the five awareness levels you classify against, and the exact shape of the brief you hand the owner.

Write the brief into the owner's own folder as a markdown file, for example `ad-review/creative-brief-YYYY-MM-DD.md`, then show it to them.

## The five awareness levels

This is the evergreen ad framework. It sorts an audience by how much they already know, and it tells you what an ad has to do at each stop. Classify every existing ad against it before you write anything.

**1. Completely unaware, roughly half the market.**
They do not know they have a problem. They are scrolling, not searching.
The ad's job: interrupt. Make them question something they assumed was fine.
Hook emotion: curiosity or surprise.
Hook shapes that work: native content that does not look like an ad, or a hidden threat ("the quiet way people in this business lose money every month without noticing").
Judge it by: how many people stop and keep watching.

**2. Problem aware, roughly a fifth of the market.**
They feel the pain. They do not know what is causing it or how to fix it.
The ad's job: name the pain out loud, then show what it costs if it stays.
Hook emotion: frustration.
Hook shapes that work: "still stuck with this?", "most people never realise what is actually causing this", the three mistakes breakdown.
Judge it by: clicks and opt ins.

**3. Solution aware, roughly a sixth of the market.**
They know fixes exist. They are comparing types of fix.
The ad's job: position your method as the smartest of the options.
Hook emotion: hope and clarity.
Hook shapes that work: "the fastest way to get there is not the obvious one", the comparison, the method walkthrough.
Judge it by: clicks and opt ins.

**4. Product aware, roughly a tenth of the market.**
They know you and they know your competitors. They are hunting for proof.
The ad's job: remove the risk. Show the receipts.
Hook emotion: confidence.
Hook shapes that work: the before and after with real numbers, the client case study, the reason people pick you over the alternative.
Judge it by: booked calls and purchases.

**5. Most aware, the small remainder.**
They already trust you. They are waiting for a reason to move.
The ad's job: give a clear offer with a deadline.
Hook emotion: urgency.
Hook shapes that work: the specific offer with an end date, the spots remaining, the bonus that expires.
Judge it by: booked calls and purchases.

**The strategic note that matters most.** The bottom three levels are where every credible competitor with a bigger budget is already fighting. The top two levels hold about seventy percent of the market and far less competition. When an account is starved anywhere, it is usually starved at the top.

## The brief, part by part

### Part 0. The decision card (one screen, first)

Fill this card with verified facts. Repeat the concept card once per concept, using its number from the brief.

```text
Account · date · creatives watched and share of window spend · market crowded or not
The account in one sentence

Concept number. Name (awareness level) · how many and which format
Why: the reason, with the one number that justifies it
Needs from the owner: the confirmed input or asset, or none

Fix first in the live account
Up to three findings for the owner to act on

This is N creatives across M concepts. Read the card and tell me: approve all, or tell me which numbers to change. Nothing gets generated until you say go.
```

Fill N and M with the approved batch size and concept count before showing the card.

The card is what the owner approves. Parts 1 to 5 are the evidence and sit underneath. If the card does not fit on one phone screen, cut words, not concepts.

### Missing facts (ask before the brief is shown)

Ask the owner for any fact the ads need that you do not have:

- The offer.
- The price.
- The date and time.
- The venue.
- Seats or stock.
- The deadline.
- The exact claim or qualification line.
- The call to action.

A brief shown to the owner contains no placeholders. If a fact is unknown, the brief is not ready.

### Part 1. Account snapshot

For each of the five levels, in order, write:

- **What is running.** How many ads, image or video, and the concepts in one line each.
- **What is winning.** Name the ad, the concept, and the number that makes it a winner (cost per result, and what results it produced).
- **What has gone stale.** Name the ad and show the reason: cost per result climbing while frequency climbs. Say how long it ran.
- **What has never been tried here.**

If a level has nothing running, write "nothing running here" and leave it visible. The empty rows are the most useful part of this page.

Then one short paragraph: across the whole account, what is the pattern. Example shape: "Nine of your eleven running ads speak to people who already know you. The cheapest result in the account came from the one video that opened on a problem, not on the offer."

### Part 2. Coverage gaps

Three to five lines. Which levels are starved, which are crowded, and which angle keeps getting repeated in different clothes.

Say the thing plainly. "You have four ads that are the same testimonial in four edits" is a finding. "Your creative mix could be more diverse" is not.

### Part 3. How crowded is the market

One short paragraph, and it changes the whole batch.

- **Uncrowded market.** Few credible competitors saying the same thing. Simple ads are fine here. A plain image with a clear headline can carry it. Do not overbuild.
- **Crowded market.** Everyone is running the same promise with the same stock proof. The creative has to work harder: a stronger opening three seconds, a sharper angle, a format the feed is not full of.

Say which one this account is in and give the reason you think so, from what you saw while reviewing the ads and what you know of their market.

### Part 4. The next batch

The batch size is an input, not a fixed number. If the owner names a size, use it. If not, default to ten concepts with five variations each as image ads, fifty images, plus five to ten short video hooks on the strongest concepts. A first run on a small account can be five concepts by five; say which you chose and why in one line.

Spread the concepts across the levels the snapshot says are starved. Do not put all concepts on the level that is already crowded.

For each concept, write this block:

- **Concept name.** Short and memorable, so it can be tracked later.
- **Awareness level.** Which of the five, and why this account needs it now.
- **Angle.** The one idea the ad is built on, in one sentence.
- **Hook.** The opening three seconds, written out. Both halves: what is on screen, and the first line spoken or printed. Write the line word for word, not a description of it.
- **Copy or script direction.** For a video, the beats in order. For an image, the on-image headline and the primary text direction.
- **Format.** Image or video. Say why. Image ads are first class here. They are quick to make, quick to test, and they win often.
- **Visual format.** Simple or designed, and why. Default simple.
- **Words on the asset.** The exact headline, price line and date line. These are laid on last, never generated.
- **Length.** For video, in seconds. See the length rule below.
- **What it is testing.** One line, so the tracker note writes itself later.
- **Evidence.** The thing in the account that made you recommend this. A winner to lean into, a gap to fill, or a fatigued ad to replace.

Then list the five variations for that concept. A variation changes one thing so you learn something from the result:

1. Hook line changed, everything else the same.
2. Opening visual changed.
3. Proof element swapped (a different number, a different screenshot, a different face).
4. Call to action changed.
5. A shorter cut of the same idea.

That is a starting set, not a law. If the concept calls for different variables, use different ones, and say what each variation is changing.

### The length rule

A one minute ad is a habit, not a rule. Nobody made it up on purpose.

Fifteen, twenty, and thirty seconds are all perfectly good ad lengths, and they are cheaper to make and quicker to test. Ask what the ad actually needs before defaulting to long. Seedance, for example, caps at thirty seconds, and its unlimited mode caps at twenty, so writing for fifteen to thirty seconds keeps everything easy to produce whatever generator you use.

State the length in the brief for every video concept.

### Part 5. What this batch deliberately leaves out

Short list. Angles the tracker says already burned out, headlines already used, and any level you chose not to feed this round with the reason.

This part stops the owner asking "why did you skip the obvious one", and it stops the next run from re-running a dead angle.

### Part 6. The approval question

End the brief with this, in plain words:

> This is N creatives across M concepts. Read the card and tell me: approve all, or tell me which numbers to change. Nothing gets generated until you say go.

Then stop. Wait for the answer. Do not open a generator, do not write prompts, do not produce a single image while the answer is still pending.

## Writing rules for the brief

- **No placeholders, ever.** Missing facts are asked first.
- **Every claim traces to the account.** If you did not see it while reviewing the ads or read it in the tracker, it does not go in the brief.
- **Numbers, not adjectives.** Cost per result, spend, click through rate, frequency, days running.
- **Hooks written out in full.** A hook described is a hook not yet written. Write the actual line.
- **No invented performance.** If a number is missing, say it is missing.
- **Short sentences.** The owner is reading this on a phone between other things.
- **Fits on a few screens.** If the snapshot is running long, tighten the descriptions, not the evidence.
