# The Diagnostic Spine: Market Awareness + Market Sophistication

> This is the engine room of the skill. Read it before scoring or rewriting. Almost every weak headline fails here first: it talks to a person who is not in the room.

The whole method rests on two questions you must answer before judging a single word of a headline:

1. **How much does the reader already know?** → **Awareness** (a fact about the *person*)
2. **How much has this market already been pitched?** → **Sophistication** (a fact about the *market*)

These are two independent dials. A reader can know your product cold and still sit in a market so flooded with claims that nothing moves them. Another can be totally blind to their problem while sitting in a fresh market where one plain sentence would win, if only you could reach them.

You need both dials, every time. Awareness tells you **what to say first**. Sophistication tells you **what they will still believe**.

---

## Dial 1: The Awareness Pyramid (what the reader knows)

The headline is governed by awareness. Start the headline exactly where the reader stands. Not one level above, not one level below.

### Level 5: Completely Unaware
- **Their thought:** "I don't even know I have a problem."
- **Headline must:** enter indirectly. Give words to a hidden dream, name an unspoken fear, lead from something they already agree with, or hold up an identity they want. Never lead with the product or even the problem.
- **Example:** "Do you have the courage to earn half a million dollars a year?"
- **Usually:** deep-cold interruption traffic (cold social feeds, pre-roll, display).

### Level 4: Problem Aware
- **Their thought:** "Something is wrong, but I don't know what to do about it."
- **Headline must:** name and agitate the problem in their own words. Make them feel seen.
- **Example:** "Tired of lying awake at 3am doing maths on money that isn't there?"
- **Usually:** upper-cold traffic. The problem-agitate-solve structure lives here.

### Level 3: Solution Aware
- **Their thought:** "I know solutions exist. I don't know about yours."
- **Headline must:** lead with the desire or the result, not the product name. They are shopping the outcome.
- **Example:** "Finally fill your calendar with sales calls without cold outreach."
- **Usually:** warm traffic that does not know you yet (searchers, how-to viewers).

### Level 2: Product Aware
- **Their thought:** "I know your thing exists. I'm not convinced."
- **Headline must:** reinforce desire, add fresh proof, sharpen the picture, or remove the one objection holding them back.
- **Example:** "Why 4,000 agency owners switched to this in the last 90 days."
- **Usually:** warm traffic that knows you (email list, retargeting, repeat visitors).

### Level 1: Most Aware
- **Their thought:** "I want it. I just need the deal."
- **Headline must:** state the product and the offer. Do not get creative. Do not tell a story.
- **Example:** "The Headline Pack - 40% off, ends Friday."
- **Usually:** hot traffic (cart, existing customers, promo to your own people).

### The dynamic that catches people out
Markets *move*. Your own good Level-5 ad turns its readers into Level-4 prospects, who now need a different headline. Several levels live in one market at once, which is why one headline can never serve everyone. **Mismatched awareness is the single most common reason a headline dies.**

## Dial 2: The Sophistication Spectrum (what the market has heard)

Sophistication is a property of the *market*, not the person. It decides what *kind of claim* will still be believed. The move escalates predictably: claim → bigger claim → mechanism → bigger mechanism → identity.

### Stage 1: First to market
- **State of play:** nobody has made this claim before.
- **Headline move:** be plain and direct. State the claim, dramatize it.
- **Example:** "Now! Lose ugly fat."
- Rare in 2026, but happens with a brand-new tech, rule change, or niche.

### Stage 2: Second wave
- **State of play:** competitors are now making the same claim.
- **Headline move:** keep the claim but enlarge it. Outbid on size, speed, specificity, proof. "Lose weight" becomes "Lose 20 lbs in 30 days."
- **Ceiling:** claims eventually grow past belief. That is the death of Stage 2.

### Stage 3: Mechanism
- **State of play:** the market has heard every claim and stopped believing them.
- **Headline move:** stop shouting WHAT and reveal HOW. Lead with a new mechanism that makes the old promise work again.
- **Example:** "Floats fat right out of your body." Same result, new reason to believe.
- **This is where most markets sit.** When the claim is tired, a fresh mechanism resets belief.

### Stage 4: Mechanism elaboration
- **State of play:** competitors have copied your mechanism too.
- **Headline move:** enlarge, brand, and prove the mechanism. Make it easier, faster, more certain. "The *Turbo* Keto Method." Stack proof on the mechanism itself.

### Stage 5: Market exhaustion
- **State of play:** the market disbelieves every claim and every mechanism.
- **Headline move:** drop claims entirely. Sell identity. Show them a role they want to step into.
- **Example:** the Marlboro Man. Apple's "Think Different." No claim, just a tribe.
- The hardest to write, the most powerful when it lands.

### The cigarette arc (one real market walking all five stages)
1. "I'd walk a mile for a Camel" (claim)
2. "Light up a Lucky and you won't miss the sweets" (bigger claim)
3. "Luckies - they're toasted" (mechanism)
4. "Nine out of ten doctors prefer Luckies" (elaborated mechanism + proof)
5. The Marlboro Man (identity)

Every market walks this arc. The only variable is speed.

## Crossing the two dials

The rule that separates pros from amateurs:

> **The headline is governed by awareness. The body copy is governed by sophistication.**

So a single page can run a Level-4 problem-agitation headline (to grab the right person) over Stage-3 mechanism body copy (to give them evidence they will actually accept). The headline grabs; the body proves.

| Awareness → headline leads with | Sophistication → claim must be |
|---|---|
| L1 Most Aware: product + deal | S1: plain claim |
| L2 Product Aware: benefit + proof | S2: enlarged claim |
| L3 Solution Aware: the result/desire | S3: a mechanism (how) |
| L4 Problem Aware: the problem, named | S4: branded, proven mechanism |
| L5 Unaware: dream / fear / identity | S5: identity, not claim |

Cross your two reads. That intersection is the strategy the headline has to execute.

## How to DETECT the two dials (because your student won't know these terms)

The reader of this skill is usually a business owner, not a copywriter. Do not ask them "what awareness level are you at." Infer it from plain questions.

**To read awareness, ask / look at:**
- *Where is the traffic coming from?*
  - Cold ad to people who never heard of them → likely L4–L5
  - Google / YouTube search for a solution → likely L3
  - Their email list or retargeting → likely L2
  - Existing customers / cart → L1
- *Was the click driven by an ad?* If yes, nudge the page read one level more aware than the ad itself, because the visitor self-selected by clicking (the Split Headline Rule: the ad and the page are two halves of one headline). If there is no ad in the path (warm list, organic search, direct link), use the traffic-source cue straight with no step-up.
- *Does the reader already know they have this problem?* No → L5. Yes, but not shopping → L4. Actively shopping a fix → L3.
- *Does the page name the product/brand in the promise?* If yes but traffic is cold, that is an awareness mismatch (a classic error).

**To read sophistication, ask / look at:**
- *How many competitors are selling this same promise?* None → S1. A few → S2. Everyone, and buyers are skeptical → S3. Everyone has a "method/system/framework" too → S4. The market rolls its eyes at all of it → S5.
- *Has the reader tried other solutions and been burned?* Burned and cynical → S4–S5. Fresh and hopeful → S1–S2.
- A quick proxy: search the niche. If the first page of ads all promise the same outcome, you are at least S3.

When you cannot tell, state your best guess, say what would change it, and proceed. Do not stall.

---

## Two lenses to carry into the scoring

- **The promise-strength lens.** A headline's promise is stronger when it raises the **outcome the reader dreams of** and the **believability that it will happen for them**, while lowering the **time it takes** and the **effort it demands**. When comparing two rewrites that both fit the diagnosis, prefer the one that wins on more of these four levers.
- **Markets are living things.** Your own ads age the market - every successful campaign moves its readers up an awareness level and the whole niche up the sophistication arc. Re-diagnose on every new test; last quarter's read may already be stale.

Teach all of this in plain words as the program's method. Do not name outside gurus, books, or programs when presenting it.
