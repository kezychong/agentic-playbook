# The Principle Layer: the deeper method behind the scores

> Read this before Step 5 of the analysis flow (desire diagnosis), and again when generating
> rewrites in Step 8. The rubric tells you WHAT to score; this file is the accumulated
> split-tested knowledge of WHY those things win, plus the working moves for fixing them.
> Everything here is distilled from decades of measured direct-response testing. Teach it in
> plain words as the program's method.

---

## 1. The one-controlling-concept test

A headline (and the page under it) carries exactly ONE idea. Not several benefits competing.

- **The one-sentence test.** State the headline's core promise in one sentence. If you can't,
  or it takes two sentences to cover everything it's trying to say, it's carrying too much.
  When two candidate headlines each push a different promise, that's a test to run - never a
  reason to merge them into one crowded line.
- **The two-second read.** A stranger skimming for two seconds should get all three at once:
  this is *important* (to them), *exciting*, and *beneficial* (to them specifically). If they
  have to work to parse it, it fails regardless of how clever it is.
- **Simplicity × stakes.** The strongest concepts sit at a sweet spot: simple enough to say
  cleanly in one line, AND mattering enough to be worth the reader's attention. Clever-but-
  trivial underperforms. So does important-but-tangled. Push every rewrite toward both at once.
- **The say-it-out-loud filter.** Explain the headline's idea to another person without
  re-reading it. If you stumble or over-explain, the idea isn't landing cleanly. A great
  concept feels obviously good the moment it's heard - it never needs to be argued for.
- **The cold-reader check.** Read it as someone who, five seconds ago, was doing something
  totally unrelated. No context, no goodwill. If it doesn't grab THAT person, it's pitched at
  the wrong awareness or attention level for cold traffic.
- **Pull = curiosity × benefit.** A headline's pull comes from two ingredients multiplied:
  an open loop the reader wants closed, AND a clear payoff for closing it. Curiosity with no
  payoff is clickbait; payoff with no open loop is a label. The winners carry both.
- **Emotional relevance beats raw size.** A claim can be objectively huge and still convert
  poorly if it doesn't land inside a want or fear the reader already carries. "Impressive" is
  not the bar; "lands on a live nerve" is. A smaller claim on a real nerve beats a dramatic
  claim that's generic.

## 2. The eight primal drives (and how to pick the desire)

Desire cannot be manufactured - only found and channeled. These eight hardwired drives are
stronger and more reliable than any learned preference:

1. Staying alive and safe (survival, health, longevity)
2. Food and drink (and enjoyment of them)
3. Escaping fear, pain, and danger
4. Romantic and sexual companionship
5. Comfortable surroundings and living conditions
6. Status - winning, being superior, keeping up with or beating others
7. Protecting and caring for the people they love
8. Approval and acceptance from others

A headline anchored to one of these tends to outperform one anchored to a milder learned want
(convenience, tidiness, mild curiosity). When a headline is clear and specific but still feels
flat, run this checklist - flatness usually means no primal root.

**Choosing between candidate desires:**

- **Rank by pull.** Score each candidate desire on three things: how *urgent* it feels right
  now, how *durable and recurring* it is (not a passing novelty), and how *widely shared* it is
  across the audience. Anchor the headline to the highest-ranking one, not merely a true one.
- **Find what they never tire of.** Most audiences have ONE desire they respond to over and
  over without fatigue - visible in what already converts (the ledger shows it). Once found,
  build variants around that same desire instead of scattering tests across unrelated appeals.
  When a variant loses, check whether it drifted off this recurring desire.
- **Make the hope-or-fear call once per market.** Diagnose whether the whole market runs on
  moving TOWARD a better future (aspiration) or AWAY from a loss (fear/dissatisfaction). Make
  that call once, then keep every headline consistent with it - don't re-decide line by line.
- **Problem-led vs aspiration-led.** If the reader has an acute, currently-felt pain, open with
  the problem and make its cost concrete. If the audience is purely aspirational with no acute
  pain, do NOT force a problem in - it reads hollow. Open with the desired future state.
- **Pain-frame as the default challenger.** Escaping a negative usually out-pulls gaining the
  matching positive. Whenever you write a pleasure-framed variant, write the pain-framed twin
  and put it in the test.
- **Reach for the sharpest emotion word.** A precise, high-intensity feeling word beats a vague
  mild one ("dread the Monday inbox" beats "feel less stressed"). This is a word-level polish - 
  run it after the concept and desire are already right.
- **Regulated niches have a word budget.** In compliance-heavy or trust-sensitive niches
  (finance, health), keep emotional language to the known-safe register for that platform and
  market before judging the headline on anything else. A brilliant line that can't run is a 0.

## 3. The opening-angle taxonomy (pick the angle before the words)

Every headline takes one of a small set of angles, and the right family is set by awareness:

- **Direct angles** - state the offer, the promise, or the problem-and-fix openly. For readers
  who already know they want this outcome (solution-aware and warmer, L1-L3).
- **Indirect angles** - pull the reader in through a teased secret, a bold declarative claim,
  or a short story. For readers who don't yet know they want it (L4-L5).

Decades of independent testing converge on this same split - it's durable, not a style choice.

**The working menu of angle families** (pit these against each other in tests): the
secret/hidden-knowledge angle · the direct-promise angle · the pure-curiosity angle · the
emotional-story angle · the value-first angle (lead with the tangible thing they get) · the
topical angle (tied to something happening right now) · the contrarian angle (against the
market's common advice). When Mode B says "big swing," it means jump FAMILIES, not reword
within one.

**Three micro-shapes worth testing on any opt-in page:** the before/after time snapshot ("from
[state X] to [state Y] in [timeframe]") · the two-similar-people contrast (same start, only one
has the edge) · the quiz/diagnostic question opening.

**The self-identification scene.** For jaded audiences numb to direct benefit claims: describe
one ultra-specific, emotionally real moment - so precise the right reader thinks "that's me" - 
with no explicit promise attached. The recognition IS the hook.

**Four questions that surface the angle:** What's genuinely NEW to this reader? What's their
single biggest problem? Their single biggest dream? And what's the boldest claim this specific
audience won't roll their eyes at (given everything they've already heard - the sophistication
dial)? The strongest answer becomes the angle.

**Curiosity needs a real anchor.** A teased secret only works when something real and specific
sits behind the tease - a real mechanism, real proof, a real story. Curiosity with nothing
behind it reads as clickbait, scores down, and burns trust.

## 4. Concreteness moves (make it land: and keep it true)

- **Direct benefit beats implied benefit.** "Relief" and "results" gesture at an outcome;
  name the literal thing the reader will be able to do or have. Flag every abstract benefit
  word and replace it with the actual experience.
- **Turn numbers into felt comparisons.** A raw figure reads as information; translated into
  something the reader can picture ("enough to cover a year of school fees"), the same true
  number registers emotionally. Use when a statistic isn't landing.
- **Dial up REAL stakes.** State the actual consequence of acting or not acting concretely
  enough to picture, instead of a flat summary. True but mild phrasing wastes a real stake.
- **The one-line metaphor swap.** Replace an abstract description with a fitting comparison.
  Generate several; keep only one that's accurate AND instantly vivid. A metaphor that needs
  explaining defeats its purpose in a one-line format.
- **Exaggerate one trait - sparingly.** Pushing a single attribute slightly past realistic
  proportion can make a line memorable, but only where the exaggeration still points honestly
  at the true claim. It magnifies one trait; it never inflates the promise.
- **Vividness must stay true.** Only sharpen claims that are already true. Penalize any
  specificity that can't be traced to something real about the offer. "More vivid" and "more
  accurate" are one constraint, not a trade-off. (This is the program's honesty rule restated
  at the technique level.)
- **Spend vividness where it counts.** The headline is the single highest-leverage line on the
  page; give it the full concrete treatment before polishing anything below it.
- **Route a boring fact through a hot button.** A flat factual claim converts better re-arrived
  at through a concern the reader is already emotionally tuned into. The fact stays honest;
  what changes is the door it walks in through.
- **The six-element construction check.** The strongest headlines activate several of:
  specificity (real numbers) · a fresh-mechanism implication (not a tired cliché) · credibility
  (a named source) · social proof (results for people like them) · juxtaposition (the fix from
  an unexpected source) · controversy (framed as news someone would rather keep quiet). Count
  the active elements when scoring; add one when rewriting.
- **Own the sharpest claim.** When competitors could technically say the same thing, the first
  to state one sharp, specific, ownable version (a concrete number, a concrete guarantee) owns
  it in the reader's mind. A generic value-stack loses to one owned claim.
- **Name the reader before the ask.** A common opt-in page failure: grabby line straight to
  the button, with nobody ever told who this is FOR. The headline or the line under it should
  call out the reader ("for the [specific person] who wants [outcome]") before the CTA.
- **The subhead-alone test.** Cover the headline; read the subhead alone. It should still be a
  complete, compelling statement. Then reverse it. Each line earns its place independently.

## 5. When you're stuck generating candidates

- **Mine real reader language.** Pull the exact words the audience uses about their situation
  (reviews, forums, comments, support emails) and build the line from THEIR phrasing. Mined
  language feels "written for me"; paraphrase feels like marketing.
- **Mine the body copy.** The strongest line on the page is often buried mid-page. Comb what's
  already written for its most compelling sentence and promote it to headline candidate.
- **Reverse-engineer winners into skeletons.** Take a proven headline (yours or the ledger's
  past winners), strip the specifics to a bare structure with blanks, refill with this offer's
  specifics. Fast candidates, proven bones. (The formula library is exactly this, pre-built.)
- **Collide facts with outside ideas.** Pair one specific fact about the product or audience
  with an unrelated idea and hunt for the connection. Let the combination sit before judging - 
  the useful link is rarely the first one. This is how fresh angles are made on purpose.
- **Invent, don't recite.** A headline that restates a feature or research finding verbatim is
  raw material posing as a finished line. Facts are inputs; the angle is the work. If every
  candidate so far sounds like a feature list read aloud, this is the missing move.

## 6. Judging clicks (carry into Mode B)

- **Qualified clicks beat volume.** When the job is an opt-in, judge the headline on whether it
  attracts the RIGHT reader - fewer, better-qualified opt-ins can beat a bigger number of wrong
  ones (the back-end numbers settle it).
- **Test the opening against one unchanged page.** The headline + opening carries the large
  majority of a page's conversion power, so test multiple openings over one constant body - 
  never rewrite the whole page per variant.
- **The cheap pre-test.** When two concepts feel equally strong on paper, run small, cheap ad
  traffic on the candidate lines and compare click-through against a known baseline before
  building anything around either. The market answers for pennies what a debate never settles.
