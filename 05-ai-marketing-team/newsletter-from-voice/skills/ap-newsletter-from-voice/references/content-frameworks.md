# Content frameworks

These are rebuilt writing recipes with generic credit.
Use one per issue, chosen by the member or their saved default.
A recipe organizes real material; it cannot create missing experience or evidence.
Start with the reader's existing concern and desired change.
Match what they already understand, then offer one useful next step.
The examples below are invented teaching examples about fictional coach Sam.
Never use their dates, events, or results as member evidence.

## 1. Problem → Importance → Experience → Lesson
Credit: a proven creator writing framework, rebuilt for a teaching issue.
Use when: the writer has lived through a problem the reader recognizes.
Beats, in order:
1. Name the reader's problem in words supported by their actual questions.
2. Explain why it matters now without inventing urgency.
3. Give the writer's specific, dated experience with that problem.
4. Draw one lesson that experience can support.
5. End with one small ask.
Subject rules: name the friction or its surprising cause; promise the lesson the body delivers.
Worked example, fictional coach Sam:
- Reader evidence: subscribers requested a weekly planning worksheet on 2 September 2026.
- Subject: Why the easy task came first
- Problem: Why does the easiest task win a busy morning?
- Importance: It can fill the space meant for work that matters.
- Experience: On 3 September, I answered messages before outlining my workshop.
- Experience: By lunch, my outline still had only a title.
- Lesson: I now write one outline heading before opening messages.
- Ask: Choose the first heading for your next work session.
Failure modes:
- Inventing a painful moment the audience never described.
- Dressing a general opinion as a dated personal event.
- Adding three lessons and three different asks.

## 2. Story issue
Credit: a classic story-email style, rebuilt around one earned lesson.
Use when: a true scene contains a change in understanding.
Beats, in order:
1. Open mid-scene with a detail that raises a reader-relevant question.
2. Follow one event and its tension without side stories.
3. Show the turn that changed the writer's understanding.
4. State the lesson the scene earns.
5. Make one soft ask tied to that lesson.
Subject rules: point to the scene's unresolved detail; resolve it within the opening two lines.
Worked example, fictional coach Sam:
- Reader evidence: subscribers requested a workshop planning note on 2 September 2026.
- Subject: The blank square on my plan
- Scene: My workshop plan had a blank square where the first exercise belonged.
- Tension: I kept adding slides around it.
- Turn: Then I tried explaining the exercise aloud and heard the missing instruction.
- Lesson: A neat plan can hide a step nobody understands yet.
- Ask: Say your first instruction aloud before adding another slide.
Failure modes:
- Opening with a biography instead of a scene.
- Inventing dialogue or another person's private details.
- Attaching a lesson or offer the story never supports.

## 3. One idea, one proof, one ask
Credit: a concise value-email pattern, rebuilt around inspectable evidence.
Use when: one claim has one strong receipt and needs little backstory.
Beats, in order:
1. State one narrow claim that matters to this reader.
2. Show its receipt, such as an owned screenshot, measured number, or the writer's own recorded words.
3. Explain the receipt's limit, then make one related ask.
Subject rules: name the concrete change or proof; never promise more than the receipt demonstrates.
Worked example, fictional coach Sam:
- Reader evidence: subscribers requested an appointment checklist on 2 September 2026.
- Subject: One question removed a form step
- Idea: One clear question replaced a separate box on my appointment form.
- Proof: My 3 September drafts show four fields before the change and three after it.
- Limit: That proves a shorter form, not more bookings.
- Ask: Check whether two fields on your form request the same information.
Failure modes:
- Using a claimed number without the underlying record.
- Treating a single observation as proof of a universal result.
- Adding an unrelated sale after the practical ask.

## 4. This week I did X
Credit: a documented-work publishing pattern, rebuilt around reader usefulness.
Use when: something was actually built and its effect can be shown.
Beats, in order:
1. Name the completed thing through a reader-relevant curiosity.
2. Show what changed, with before-and-after evidence and its limits.
3. Explain what the reader can copy as one action.
Subject rules: name the built object or changed friction; avoid an empty weekly-update label.
Worked example, fictional coach Sam:
- Reader evidence: subscribers requested a workshop packing checklist on 2 September 2026.
- Subject: My bag now has a paper lid
- Built: A paper checklist now sits on top of my packed workshop bag.
- Change: On 4 September, it caught the missing marker before I closed the bag.
- Limit: That happened once; I have no longer-term result yet.
- Copy and ask: Put your packing list where closing the bag requires seeing it.
Failure modes:
- Reporting busywork with no reader benefit.
- Calling a plan a completed build.
- Claiming measured results before enough evidence exists.
