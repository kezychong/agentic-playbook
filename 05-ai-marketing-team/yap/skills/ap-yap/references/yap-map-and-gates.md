# Build and judge the yap map

## Start with truth

Ask two to four questions. Find the real event, the member's view, evidence, and who feels the consequence.
Reflect the answers back and wait for confirmation before using them as the member's story.
Never invent facts, stories, numbers, results, or feelings. Do not fact-check a lived story.
Check outside facts where possible and flag them "check this" so the member can see what needs attention.

## Script the edges, bullet the middle

A yap map has exact opening lines and an exact close. The middle has five short bullet beats.
The member speaks through those beats in their own words. Do not turn the middle into paragraphs to read aloud.

| Beat | What goes here | Why they keep watching |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | A claim or confession | It gives them a clear point to consider |
| Tension | Why it matters or what went wrong | They recognise a problem with a consequence |
| Insight | The pattern the member noticed | They want to understand the pattern |
| Evidence | A confirmed event, number, or cost | They can see what supports the claim |
| Path | What the viewer should do or avoid | They can apply the point to their own life |

Write a specific watching reason for each beat in the actual map. Use the table as guidance, not fixed copy.

## Keep it raw

Leave three to five seconds of silence or visible processing before the opening. Keep it natural.
Use a bold on-screen text line that opens a question in the viewer's mind.
Carry a real emotional state the member confirmed, such as concern, conviction, shock, or disbelief.
Keep one fight per video. A fight means the single claim or tension the video explores.
Check the underlying chain: claim, proof, meaning, viewer implication.
Do not force outrage or turn a real feeling into a performance.

## Run the four taste gates

| Gate | Pass condition | Repair when it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Real fight | A clear claim or tension drives the video | Choose the central tension and cut competing fights |
| Viewer sees themselves | The viewer recognises their own situation | Replace self-praise with the confirmed consequence for the viewer |
| Proof | Confirmed evidence supports the claim | Ask for a real event, number, or cost; never make one up |
| Member's voice | The words sound like the member | Use their wording and remove borrowed rhythms or fake outrage |

Avoid openings such as "here's the secret" and canned lists of hacks.
Run all gates before handing over a map. Rewrite a failure, then check again.
Tell the member which gate you fixed and the change you made. If none needed repairs, say all gates passed.
An unresolved truth gap goes back to the interview. It cannot pass by adding confident language.

## Shape the opening and close

Use a pattern many top short-form creators use:

- Start with a spoken claim, without a long setup.
- Add a short text hook that complements the spoken line.
- Put proof right after the opening. Develop it further in the Evidence beat.
- Give the viewer a path they can follow.
- Give the idea a plain name so the viewer has a reason to stay to the end.

This shapes the edges and the delivery order. Keep the middle's five beats and their watching notes intact.
