# Back End — Where the Money Compounds

The challenge is priced cheap on purpose. The return isn't the front-end sale — it's the **ascension** into an offer you can actually fulfil. Design this before you launch, so the challenge content builds toward it.

Legend: **[the course presenter]** = his call. **[RESEARCHED]** = credited benchmarks/patterns.

## The core move

**[the course presenter]** After the challenge ends, upsell graduates into **something you can fulfil.** The three natural destinations:

1. **Coaching** (1:1) — highest touch, highest price, but capacity-limited. If the audience is overseas / timezone-limited, 1:1 may be the wrong fit — this is often the exact bottleneck the challenge was built to escape, so don't ascend people back into it.
2. **Group coaching** — the usual sweet spot: leveraged, still high-touch, no per-person calendar drain.
3. **Monthly membership / community** — a Skool-style continuity offer. The natural long-term step; recurring revenue instead of one-time.

## Choosing the destination

Match the back end to what the user can fulfil at scale (from `offer.md`):
- Full calendar + timezone limits → **group or membership**, not 1:1.
- Wants recurring revenue → **membership/continuity.**
- Has room for high-touch and a premium price → **group coaching** (or selective 1:1).

## If the back end is a membership (continuity)

**[the course presenter]** A Skool-style community is a strong continuity vehicle — "you could even do Skool like me." But note his honesty: he's "still learning" it and not yet an expert. The hard part of a membership is **retention, not content.** His method, worth passing on:

- **Study successful communities in the niche before building one.** the course presenter buys into thriving Skools specifically to see *how they run and retain*, not for their content (he notes a lot of it is basic). Example he cites: a swimming coach's "Superhero Swim University" — a paid Skool (~$38/mo, ~300 members) proving even a low-pain niche can retain. If a swim coach can keep members, a high-pain niche (health, money) can too.
- **Free tier as the attractor, paid tier as the offer** — the swim coach runs a free community as a lead magnet and a paid one as the real product.
- **Pain markets retain more easily.** If the audience is in genuine pain (diabetes, debt, relationships), retention is easier than in a nice-to-have niche.

## Timing the pitch (seed it before the end)

**[RESEARCHED — sources disagree, both valid]:**
- **60% Rule** (*Scale For Impact*): introduce the offer ~60% through, then run a sales sequence to close + post-challenge nurture.
- **Bridge + offer day** (*CommuniPass, 9-day model*): a bridge day connecting the challenge win to the bigger transformation, then an offer day with a 24-hour cart + bonuses.
- Both agree on the failure mode: **don't wait for the last day and pitch cold for 10 minutes** (~1–2% conversion). Seed early, close deliberately.

## What to realistically expect (benchmarks)

**[RESEARCHED]** conversion from a well-run challenge into the back end, by back-end price (*Scale For Impact, CommuniPass*):
- $47 challenge → **~$2K offer: 10–30%** (well-run).
- By ticket: **$97–297 = 8–15% · $497–997 = 4–8% · $1,997–4,997 = 1–3%.**
- Cited case: $27–47 entry → $997 cohort, **6.2% of completers → $37,886 in 9 days.**

Use these to set honest revenue expectations against the user's actual completer count — not to promise a number.

## The honesty anchor

**[the course presenter]** the celebrated "$1M in 70 minutes, zero ads" launch worked because the creator had **built a large warm social audience for years first.** The challenge monetized that reach; it didn't create it. Always tie back-end projections to the user's real audience size. A challenge with strong ascension mechanics still needs people in the room — reach first, funnel second.
